Background

I started in software engineering after earning a computer science degree at DigiPen in 2011, building games and educational software. I later returned to school to study applied physics and mathematics at Coastal Carolina University, graduating in 2020.

Along the way, a research internship at CU Boulder / HAO / LASP introduced me to stellar data analysis. My Ph.D. research at UC Davis has focused on semiconductor materials and solar cell degradation, bringing together physics, numerical modeling, and software development.

Projects

Hydrogen transport model

I co-developed a model of hydrogen diffusion, trapping, and release in layered silicon solar cells. A redesigned numerical formulation reduced runtime by over 40,000 times, making large parameter sweeps practical.

GitHub ↗

Solar cell device modeling

I used SCAPS-1D and ASA simulations to connect atomic-scale defects to voltage loss in silicon heterojunction solar cells, automating simulations and analysis to study carrier recombination and band bending.

High-throughput atomistic simulations

I built a Python/LAMMPS workflow to test an oxygen-transport explanation for solar cell degradation. The pipeline automated thousands of calculations, recovered failed runs, and combined the results for analysis.

GitHub ↗

Machine-learning interatomic potentials

I built a MACE/PyTorch workflow to explore faster atomistic simulations, preparing DFT datasets and training initial models for silicon, oxygen, and hydrogen systems.

Quantum transport modeling

I optimized transfer-matrix simulations of disordered quantum-dot materials. Restructuring the linear algebra and reducing overhead made it possible to study systems about 30% larger in comparable runtime.

Stellar spectral analysis

I built an open-source Python pipeline to turn stellar spectra into time series of magnetic activity. The workflow includes quality checks and diagnostic plots, with results validated against the original Fortran calculations.
